# Educational Explainer — Visual Story Recipes

Use these patterns as starting points, not fixed templates. Choose the visual structure that makes the subject easiest to understand.

## 1. Process or sequence
Best for: how something is made, how a service works, a ritual, a workflow, or a chain of events.
- establish the starting state,
- introduce each stage as a visible action,
- carry an object, signal, or camera path between stages,
- show the result of the final stage.
Useful devices: connected path, machine with moving parts, object relay, unfolding diagram, progress through a real environment.
Avoid: six numbered cards that simply appear one after another if the process can be shown as one connected transformation.

## 2. Cause and effect
Best for: science, social topics, health education, environmental issues.
- show the initial condition,
- make the trigger visible,
- connect the trigger to the result,
- reveal secondary consequences only after the primary relationship is clear.
Useful devices: flow field, branching paths, cutaway, layered environment, causal chain.
Avoid implying causation from two unrelated facts.

## 3. System or mechanism
Best for: machines, technology, anatomy, ecosystems, institutions.
- introduce the whole,
- isolate a meaningful component,
- reveal its function,
- show how it interacts with other components,
- return to the whole so the audience sees the relationship.
Useful devices: exploded view, cutaway, orbit, x-ray layer, component callouts, color-coded signal path.

## 4. Comparison
Best for: before/after, options, old/new, scale, advantages and disadvantages.
- establish the comparison criteria,
- align comparable elements,
- reveal the difference using consistent scales and labels,
- state the insight rather than forcing the viewer to calculate it.
Useful devices: morphing shape, split composition, synchronized timelines, proportional objects, bar chart.
Never distort an axis or object scale to dramatize a result.

## 5. Scale and quantity
Best for: large numbers, distances, time, energy, population, or microscopic/macroscopic ideas.
- introduce a human-scale reference,
- change scale gradually or through a motivated transition,
- keep a stable reference when possible,
- label units and the meaning of the scale.
Useful devices: repeated units, nested worlds, zoom from macro to micro, scale comparison, counter linked to actual data.
Do not use an illustrative number as if it were sourced data.

## 6. Timeline and history
Best for: historical events, biographies, development of an idea or technology.
- establish period and location,
- move through a small number of meaningful turning points,
- show what changed between points,
- connect the ending to the opening question.
Useful devices: map journey, evolving object, archive/document collage, layered time markers, artifact transformation.
Use dates only when verified. Distinguish archival material, modern illustration, and reconstruction.

## 7. Abstract concept
Best for: ideas that have no obvious physical appearance, such as trust, memory, information, or probability.
- identify the underlying relationship,
- choose a consistent metaphor,
- define what each part of the metaphor represents,
- demonstrate the idea,
- clarify where the metaphor stops being literal.
Useful devices: containers, signals, threads, networks, particles, fields, mirrored states, modular shapes.
Do not let a beautiful metaphor create a false explanation.

## 8. Data and visual journalism
Best for: statistics, current events, public information, and evidence-led explainers.
- state the question,
- identify the source and unit,
- reveal the relevant data,
- compare or map it accurately,
- highlight the supported conclusion,
- show source notes in a readable end card or context label.
Choose charts according to the data: bars for comparisons, lines for change over time, maps for geographic patterns, and diagrams for mechanisms. Do not fake numbers or omit context.

## 9. Character-led explanation
Best for: children, daily-life education, social behavior, or stories where a human action clarifies the concept.
- introduce a recognizable situation,
- let the character perform an action,
- show the consequence,
- repeat with a changed choice or condition,
- close with a simple takeaway.
Use readable poses and clear silhouettes. Keep character acting and explanatory labels from competing for attention.

## Scene construction
A useful explainer scene often combines a primary subject with a few supporting elements: subject/entity, action or mechanism, evidence or example, scale/number/label, and annotation/context. Build these progressively when it helps comprehension. Do not treat a fixed element count as a requirement.

## Research and factual integrity
Before visual design:
1. list the claims that appear in the narration,
2. record the source for each important factual claim,
3. list named people, organizations, products, places, and events,
4. decide which entities need a visual identifier,
5. label generated reconstructions as illustrations where viewers could mistake them for documentary footage.

For factual content, avoid fabricating figures, quotes, dates, source credits, or photographs. If no reliable image exists, use a clearly illustrative visual instead of pretending it is a real photograph.

## Audience adaptation
For children and beginners: one new concept at a time, familiar objects and concrete examples, clear actions and visible cause/effect, short labels, and slower holds for new terms.
For more advanced audiences: denser diagrams are acceptable, use precise terminology, preserve nuance, and still make the main relationship clear.
