# Educational Explainer Skill

## Purpose
Explain a subject accurately and simply without making it simplistic. The viewer should see how an idea works, not just read a sequence of statements.

## Learning design
Before designing visuals, identify:
- what the audience already knows,
- what they need to learn,
- the likely misconception or point of confusion,
- the single most important takeaway,
- what evidence or source supports the explanation.

Break complex topics into small conceptual steps. Introduce terminology only when needed and explain it in plain language.

## Choose the narrative structure
Use the structure that fits the topic:
- question → answer,
- problem → cause → consequence,
- whole → parts → relationship,
- before → change → after,
- step → action → result,
- misconception → correction,
- comparison → conclusion,
- event → turning point → impact.

Do not force every topic into the same hook/problem/solution/recap formula.

## Visual teaching methods
- Use everyday analogies for abstract concepts.
- Show cause and effect as connected actions.
- Use before/after states for change.
- Use cutaways or exploded views for systems.
- Use paths and object relays for processes.
- Use maps only when geography matters.
- Use repeated units or accurately scaled diagrams to explain quantity.
- Use charts when the data supports the chart type.
- Use simple characters when human action helps explain the concept.
- Return to the full system after explaining its parts.

For a complex scene, a primary subject can be supported by evidence, labels, examples, scale, and annotations. Add elements progressively when each one contributes to the explanation. Never add objects only to make the screen busy.

See references/explainer-visual-recipes.md for detailed patterns.

## Scene and motion design
A new sentence does not always require a new scene. Let a scene develop through successive visual beats when the ideas belong together.

For each scene, define:
- the learning point,
- the visual event,
- the relation between objects,
- the voiceover beat,
- the viewer's focal point,
- what changes over time,
- the next narrative step.

Do not simply place a still illustration beside a paragraph and fade to the next illustration. Let the object, process, evidence, camera, or diagram demonstrate the idea.

## Audience adaptation

### Young children
- use familiar examples from daily life,
- prefer concrete objects over abstract symbols,
- explain one new concept at a time,
- use warm, clear language,
- make actions and consequences visible,
- hold important terms long enough to understand,
- avoid dense labels and fast transitions.

### General audiences
- explain jargon briefly,
- use examples people can recognize,
- keep the pace comfortable,
- preserve the nuance that changes the meaning.

### Knowledgeable audiences
- use precise terminology,
- allow denser diagrams where useful,
- avoid over-explaining familiar concepts,
- maintain clear visual hierarchy.

## Accuracy and trust
- Separate fact from analogy.
- Do not present a metaphor as a literal mechanism.
- Do not invent citations, statistics, quotes, historical details, or source credits.
- Record sources for important factual claims.
- Distinguish archival images, user-provided assets, generated illustrations, and reconstructions.
- Preserve context and units when presenting data.

## Retention
Repeat the central idea in a new form: a visual summary, a practical example, a transformation, or a final image that resolves the opening question.

## Quality check
Ask:
1. Could the viewer explain the idea after watching?
2. Does the visual add meaning beyond the narration?
3. Is any metaphor misleading?
4. Are the sources and data reliable?
5. Can children or beginners follow the causal steps?
6. Is the text readable at the target screen size?
7. Does the animation help understanding rather than distract?
